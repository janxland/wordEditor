# Positioning Was Visible, Leverage Was Not: Reviewing Reuters' July 2024 Coverage of the Yen Carry Trade Unwind

# Part 1: Summary

Alun John, Harry Robertson, and Tom Westbrook, writing for Reuters on 29 July 2024, set out to explain why a nine-yen rally had undone the year's most crowded currency trade (John et al., 2024). The claim is that a "perfect storm of political, policy and technical risks" hit traders who borrowed yen near zero to buy higher-yielding assets, and that the unwind was already reaching currencies with no link to Japan (John et al., 2024, para. 1).

Three claims carry the piece. The first is that official action started the move but did not finish it: nearly $40 billion of suspected intervention ignited the yen's rise, yet the currency kept appreciating without further intervention, which the authors read as private positioning taking over (John et al., 2024, paras. 2–3). The second is that the trade was already coming apart before the Bank of Japan met, since speculators had cut their bearish bets by the most since March 2020, leaving a net short of $8.61 billion (John et al., 2024, para. 6). The third is transmission: the Swiss franc and offshore yuan strengthened in the same week, and the Australian dollar fell 3.6% in a fortnight (John et al., 2024, paras. 14–18).

The most useful passage is the easiest to overlook. Nathan Swami of Citi warns that yen-funded carry trades "appear to be increasingly vulnerable to VaR shocks" (John et al., 2024, "Steamroller" section, para. 4), and Andreas Koenig of Amundi adds that currency moves "can easily wipe out any gains from carry" (John et al., 2024, para. 19).

# Part 2: Analysis

I agree with the article's central claim, though it is more radical than the authors allow. Everything they report supports reading the move as a positioning event rather than a policy event. The Bank of Japan's end-July increase was small, 15 basis points to 0.25%, and the BIS calls it merely "perceived as a hawkish rate hike" (Aquilina et al., 2024). Under interest parity, a 15-basis-point change in the rate differential should move the spot rate by roughly that much. What followed was a 14% yen appreciation in under a month and a 12% single-day fall in the TOPIX on 5 August (Aquilina et al., 2024). A 15-basis-point repricing cannot produce a 12% equity move, so the mechanism must be leverage.

That is where I part company with the framing. Its $8.61 billion net short comes from the CFTC, which sees only regulated futures and options. The BIS later put the yen-funded carry trade at roughly ¥40 trillion, or $250 billion, going into the event, and cautioned that even this is biased downward because of data gaps (Aquilina et al., 2024). The distance between those figures is not measurement error. It is categorical: most yen funding is arranged through foreign exchange swaps, which are not booked as debt. The sentence that reassured readers described a small slice of the exposure, five trading days before that exposure forced a global deleveraging.

I also disagree with the lesson the piece implies. If leverage and margin are the problem, the price that matters is not the interest rate differential but the cost of obtaining dollars through the swap market, which surfaces as a widening cross-currency basis. The IMF agrees that when uncertainty rises, hedging demand widens cross-currency bases, dealer concentration and nonbank participation amplify the pressure, and the opacity of over-the-counter derivatives lets vulnerabilities build undetected (International Monetary Fund, 2025). This is textbook second-generation crisis logic: a move becomes self-validating once enough leveraged positions share the same trigger level.

I would not treat the Japanese authorities as bystanders either, since their intervention was the trigger. Fund members may act against disorderly conditions but must not target a competitive exchange rate, and that surveillance runs country by country (International Monetary Fund, 2025). The exposure their intervention dislodged sat in the swap market and linked Japanese and Swiss funding, the offshore yuan, Mexican bonds, and United States technology equities, a chain belonging to no single country's consultation. The article appeared a week before the worst of it. Its reporting was accurate; its ordering of causes was not.
